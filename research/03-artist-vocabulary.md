## LANE 1 — connor-price (bouncy, playful trap-pop)

### (a) Sonic signature

**Tempo.** Automated analysis of the catalogue (S-01..S-05) clusters in two places:
- "Trap-count" tracks: "Spinnin" 140 BPM / B minor; "Typical Rapper" 141 BPM / D major; "GOLDEN GOOSE" 130 BPM / A major. These feel like 65–72 BPM half-time (snare on 3, 808 kick on 1 with a syncopated pickup).
- "Straight pop-rap" tracks: "Too Easy" 110 BPM / B minor; "EGOT" 105 BPM / F# minor.
- Working range for the lane: **100–112 BPM straight, or 130–145 BPM half-time trap count** (which Suno often interprets better as "half-time trap feel, 140 BPM" than as "70 BPM").
- Tunebat's danceability scores for the up-tempo cuts are very high (77–89/100) with energy 57–90, i.e. the lane is danceable even when the mood is light.

**Drums & groove.** Tight, fully quantized, on-grid programming (no live swing). Punchy, short, clicky trap kick that is often doubled by the 808; a bright, cracking snare or clap on 2 and 4 (half-time: on 3) frequently layered with a rimshot or snap; crisp closed hi-hats in 8ths with 16th-note and occasional 32nd rolls as fills; open-hat or clap stutters as pre-drop fills; finger snaps, claps and "percussion ear candy" (shakers, woodblock, rim clicks) that carry the bounce. The bounce comes from syncopated kick placement (pushing to the "and" before the downbeat), silent gaps in the drum pattern, and call-and-response between the 808 and the melodic pluck. `[infer]` from the catalogue; the only direct quote on his beat philosophy is that both he and his main producer answer "Simplicity" when asked what makes a good beat (S-06).

**Bass.** Clean, rounded 808 with a short-to-medium decay that is pitched to the chord root and often slides (glides) between notes at phrase ends; sits politely under the kick rather than distorting. Occasional sub-sine doubling. On the 100–110 BPM cuts the 808 behaves more like a bouncy "synth bass" with staccato eighth-note pickups.

**Harmony / keys / modes.** Minor keys dominate (B minor, F# minor appear repeatedly, S-01, S-04, S-05), with a few major-key "feel-good" cuts (A major, D major). Harmony is deliberately simple: 2–4 chord loops, often a single-register ostinato rather than voiced chords. Natural minor / Aeolian with pop-friendly i–VI–VII or i–iv–VII motion. `[infer]`

**Lead instruments / synth types.** Bright synth plucks (short-decay saw/square plucks with a touch of delay), mallet/bell patches (music-box, marimba, glockenspiel-style FM bells), pitched vocal-chop-style "oohs" used as hooks (avoid in instrumentals — see avoid list), whistle-register leads, playful pizzicato-string or kalimba plucks, occasional clean guitar riff or piano stab. Melodies are nursery-rhyme simple and repeated every bar, which is why the beats translate to short-form video.

**Textures & ear candy.** Risers, reverse-cymbal pickups, tape-stop / "vinyl stop" effects into drops, DJ-style record scratches, cartoonish SFX (coin / "ding" sounds), vocal-free "hey!" style stabs are replaced in our lane by percussive one-shots, filter sweeps on the pluck, stereo-ping-pong delay throws.

**Arrangement habits (the stop-start grammar).** Very short intros (0–4 bars, frequently the hook melody dry for 2 bars then full drop). Constant mutes: drums drop out for the last beat or last bar of a 4- or 8-bar phrase, then slam back; melodic layer muted for one bar while the 808 and snaps carry; full-stop "silence then drop" moments; beat switches / tempo-feel switches between sections (straight → half-time or vice versa); half-bar breakdowns before the hook; final chorus with added top percussion. Track lengths are short (2:00–2:45). Hook melody is present in almost every section, which is what makes the instrumental hold up without a vocal.

**Mix & master.** Crisp, clean, modern pop-rap: bright top end, tight (not boomy) low end, kick/808 phase-aligned, very little distortion or saturation, hard-tuned transients, wide stereo on plucks/pads with dead-center kick/808/snare, loud and dense but with dynamic contrast created by the mutes rather than by compression. Engineered in-house and mixed/mastered by a family member (S-06), which fits the "clean, DIY-polished" character rather than analogue-console warmth.

**Era / genre influences.** Early-2020s TikTok-era pop-rap; "internet-friendly rap" (S-07); beat packs described as ranging from "drum-heavy '90s boom-bap style" to "hard-hitting and minimal" (S-06); the artist's own blurbs cite hip-hop, R&B, soul and pop (S-08). Nearest commercial neighbours (for understanding, not prompting): clean pop-rap/comedic-rap producers of the 2019–2024 streaming era, mid-2010s pop-trap, light boom-bap for the storytelling cuts.

### (b) Producers / gear / techniques cited (understanding only)
- Primary producer and co-writer: Graham Stiefel ("GRAHAM" / 100graham), credited for drums, bass and production; sends 10–20-beat packs; produced the series' Canadian-Gold singles (S-06, S-09, S-10). Also known as a solo artist whose own pop-rap singles share the aesthetic.
- Artist self-engineers vocals; mixing/mastering by brother-in-law "Christian" (S-06). No published DAW/plugin list was found. `[uncertain]`
- Typical technique set for this sound (general knowledge, `[infer]`): stock-ish trap drum kits with layered claps/snaps, Serum/Vital-style pluck presets, FM bells, 808 with glide, short plate/room reverbs, ping-pong delay, aggressive sidechain-free arrangement mutes rather than pump compression.

## LANE 2 — paak-pocket (live swung drums, funk / neo-soul)

### (a) Sonic signature

**Tempo.** Catalogue analysis (S-11..S-14): "The Season / Carry Me" 79 BPM (Db major); "Heart Don't Stand a Chance" 94 BPM (G major); "Come Down" 98 BPM (Bb minor); "Tints" ~108 BPM (Db/F# minor per two conflicting sources); "Am I Wrong" is the disco/new-jack-swing outlier (~115–120, `[infer]`). Working range for the lane: **78–100 BPM, sweet spot 86–96**, with an optional 105–115 "funk/disco" variant.

**Drums & groove (the pocket).** Live-played kit, intentionally unquantized: "the beats … come from unquantized drums, deep enough in the pocket" (S-15). Snare consistently lands late (behind the beat) while the hi-hat stays fairly straight — the "Dilla Time" effect of straight and swung feels stacked at once (S-20, S-21, S-22): "Hi-hats can sit on a straight grid while kicks lean late and snares land early", "dragged and rushed at the same time" (S-21). MPC-style swing in the 54–58% range on 16ths is the usual programming analogue (S-22 cites 53–56% on 8ths as the commonly agreed Dilla figure). Kit character: dry, dampened, slightly muffled snare with a fat crack (tea-towel / wallet-on-snare sound), loose tuned kick with little click, closed hats with ghost-note 16ths, rimshot/cross-stick on verses, tambourine or shaker riding 8ths, brushed-feel ghost notes. Breakbeat / funk-break feel on the up-tempo cuts; one review ties "Am I Wrong" to a chopped vintage funk drum break layered under live playing (S-16). Fills are short, musical, played rather than programmed; drum sound on the first half of "Malibu" was praised as "purposefully sloppy crunch" and tied to the "drums need to hit hard" boom-bap school (S-17). The artist describes himself as a drummer first who looks for "where he'll sit in the groove" (S-18) and found "the pocket that worked" with lo-fi producers early on (S-19).

**Bass.** Fingerstyle electric bass guitar (round, slightly overdriven, P-bass type tone) playing syncopated 16th-note funk lines, slides and dead-note ghost notes; frequently doubled or replaced by a Minimoog-style analog synth bass (the band's bassist is credited on both bass guitar and bass synth, S-23). On the sample-based NxWorries material the bass is often the low end of the chopped soul loop plus a filtered sub.

**Harmony / chords / keys.** Neo-soul / gospel voicings: minor 9ths, major 7ths, 13ths, dominant 7#9 passing chords, chromatic planing, ii–V turnarounds borrowed from gospel. Keys: Bb minor, Db major, G major, F# minor appear in the analysed tracks (flat keys from horn/keys players; `[infer]`). Mixolydian and Dorian colour over funk vamps; Lydian pads on the dreamier cuts.

**Lead instruments.** Rhodes electric piano (tremolo, slightly overdriven), Wurlitzer, clavinet with wah/phaser on the funk cuts, Hammond-style organ swells, Moog/Prophet-style analog synth leads and portamento mono-synth melodies ("'70s synth" cited, S-24), muted funk guitar (chicken-pickin' 16ths, wah), horn section stabs (trumpet/sax/trombone — "peppy horns", "brash horns and flutes", "celebratory horns" cited across albums, S-16, S-25), flute, strings as pads or counter-melodies ("surprising sighs of strings", S-26), vibraphone, talk-box moments on the most polished album.

**Textures & ear candy.** Vinyl crackle and tape hiss on the sample-based material, chopped soul/jazz vocal-less loops, field-recording / surf-documentary snippets as interludes (S-27 — replace with non-vocal foley in our lane), tape-saturated room reverb, spring reverb on guitar, cassette wobble, crowd/party ambience (avoid — vocal risk), congas/bongos, shaker layers.

**Arrangement habits.** Live-band form: 4–8 bar instrumental intro that establishes the groove, verse/chorus with dynamic lifts from added horns or strings, bridge with a key or feel change, and the hallmark "beat switch" or coda where the band shifts to a new groove/tempo (several album tracks are two-part suites, e.g. "The Season / Carry Me"). Breakdowns to drums-and-bass only; stop-time hits; long outros with ad-lib-style instrument solos.

**Mix & master.** Warm, analogue, saturated: drums slightly compressed with a tape/console sound, vinyl-era low-mid warmth, not hyper-bright; moderate loudness with preserved transients. The three album eras differ:
- **Malibu (2016):** "raw, almost DIY feel" (S-28), "purposefully sloppy crunch" (S-17), boom-bap and "chipmunk soul" influence from guest producers (S-29), beachy / homemade. Warmest, dustiest, most sample-adjacent.
- **Oxnard (2018):** executive-produced by a famously perfectionist West Coast producer — "aggressively crisp and hard hitting", "drums cleanly punch through the mix", "trades the warmth for a harder West Coast sound", "too polished" per critics (S-25, S-30). Brightest and most hi-fi, G-funk/P-funk horns and synth bass, biggest drums.
- **Ventura (2019):** "returns to a bedroom-scale sound", "live instrumentation", "lush and sophisticated", "refined", strings and smooth 70s soul (S-31, S-32, S-33). Midpoint: warm but clean, string-laden, quiet-storm soul.
- **NxWorries (2016, 2024):** the lo-fi chop side — "subdued drums", "nourishing samples … given space", "layered sampling", Dilla/Madlib grammar (S-24, S-26, S-34); loops that are short, dusty, pitched, with heavy low-pass and tape texture; drums programmed but swung and understated.

**Era / genre influences cited.** 70s funk and soul, church/gospel drumming (S-19), Detroit MPC swing (S-20..S-22), boom bap (S-17), G-funk, new jack swing and 80s funk (S-16), Stones Throw-style lo-fi beat scene.

### (b) Producers / gear / techniques cited (understanding only)
- Guest producers across the three albums: Madlib, 9th Wonder, Kaytranada, Hi-Tek, DJ Khalil, Chris Dave & the Drumhedz, Pomo, The Alchemist, Dr. Dre (S-29, S-31, S-33). Lo-fi chop partner: Knxwledge (NxWorries) — flips short soul loops, early practice was looping church-sermon snippets to tape; MPC/SP-style sampler aesthetic (S-35, S-36).
- Live band: guitar, keys (Rhodes/Wurli/synth), bass guitar + bass synth, drums (S-23).
- Techniques (general knowledge, `[infer]`): MPC swing 54–58%, nudging snare late by 10–30 ms, hats straight; tape or console emulation on the drum bus; vinyl-sim plugins on loops; low-pass on sampled loops; parallel compression on drums to keep the "crunch" while preserving ghost notes.

## LANE 3 — jid-dark (dense dark boom-bap / trap hybrid)

### (a) Sonic signature

**Tempo.** "151 Rum" 130 BPM / E minor (S-37, S-38); "Off Deez" 150 BPM / D minor (S-39). Both are half-time trap counts (feel ≈ 65–75). Album cuts also include 85–95 BPM boom-bap/soul-sample beats and occasional double-time flows over 70–80 BPM beats. Working range: **130–160 BPM half-time trap count (65–80 BPM feel), plus an 86–96 BPM boom-bap variant.** Expect beat switches that change tempo mid-track.

**Drums & groove.** Hard, dry, mid-forward trap kick with a boom-bap-weight snare (often a layered acoustic snare + clap, slightly distorted); aggressive, busy hi-hat programming — 16th rolls, triplet bursts, off-grid hat stutters, hats panned and pitched, frequent open-hat accents on the "e" or "a"; odd-length fills and dropped beats (a 7/8 bar or a missing snare before a switch); drum switch-ups every 8–16 bars (half-time → double-time, trap → boom-bap, drums muted to leave only the sample). "Modern drums, not boom-bap drums, on soul samples" is how the executive producer describes the formative sound (S-40). Reviewers describe "a big, clomping beat with a weirdly catchy looped melody" (S-41), "off-kilter" beats (S-42), "beat and flow switches stacked" (S-43), production that "remains rhythmically and melodically complex" (S-44). The St. Louis-bounce producer on the DiCaprio-era hit is tied to "dark, heavy beats and bass" with "breathy gasps, visceral grunts, heel-pounding stomps, and wild clangs" as percussive textures (S-45, S-46).

**Bass.** Distorted, saturated 808 with long decay and slides on the trap cuts; "cushiony", minimal, sub-heavy bass (S-47) on the bounce cuts; on soul-sample cuts the bass is a filtered sample low end or a dark, round synth bass following the sample's root. Low end is dark and heavy rather than clean/pop.

**Harmony / keys / modes.** Minor keys (E minor, D minor confirmed; S-37..S-39). Harmonic minor and Phrygian colour for the menacing cuts; chopped 60s–70s soul samples (e.g. a 1965 soul record flipped on the lead single, kept "largely faithful" but "tweaked … to give it a more ominous feel", S-48) supply major-7th/minor-7th soul harmony beneath dark drums. Pitched-down samples sit in low registers, pitched-up ("chipmunk") chops supply high hooks.

**Lead instruments / samples.** Pitched/chopped vocal-sample stabs used as melodic instruments (an identifying trait — in our instrumental lane these must be described as *formant-shifted vocal-sample stabs / pitched sample chops* with "no lyrics", or replaced with pitched choir-pad synths); dusty soul and gospel loops; cinematic string swells and staccato string ostinatos; dark choir pads / mellotron-choir synths; detuned bells; eerie high synth leads; muted dark piano; brass stabs and horn swells on the grander cuts; "lambently warm … strings" compared to 70s soundtrack-soul (S-49); children's-choir and funeral-choir textures appear on the album (S-50 — vocal, so avoid literally; use "choir-pad synth" instead). Lo-fi "collage-like" dusty production on the newest album opener (S-51).

**Textures & ear candy.** Vinyl crackle, tape stop, reversed samples, filtered intros, sample "stutter" edits, risers made from reversed strings, vocal-free ad-lib SFX replaced with percussion hits, sub drops, DJ-style rewinds into switches.

**Arrangement habits.** Multi-part songs with 2–3 distinct beats (beat switches on "Stars", "Surround Sound", "Better Days", "Can't Make U Change" are highlighted, S-40); switch-ups signalled by a bar of silence or a riser; intros of 4–8 bars built on the raw sample before drums; stop-start sections where drums drop to leave only the sample and 808; final-section tempo change. Energy is "very high" (S-52); production is "grander" than earlier work (S-53). Dark Atlanta "underbelly" aesthetic on the 2025 album (S-54).

**Mix & master.** Dark, dense, mid-heavy and loud: heavy saturation on drums and 808, lo-fi filtered samples against hi-fi drums (deliberate contrast), snare crack cuts through a thick low-mid bed, hats bright and splashy, moderate width (samples wide, drums center), aggressive limiting typical of modern Atlanta rap. Not warm-and-polite — gritty.

**Era / genre influences cited.** East Atlanta rap, Dreamville-circle soul-sample rap, Southern bounce (St. Louis bounce, Memphis horrorcore influence cited by the DiCaprio-era producer, S-55), 90s boom-bap sample grammar with modern trap drums, FL Studio-era beat culture (S-56, S-57).

### (b) Producers / gear / techniques cited (understanding only)
- Executive producer and longest-running collaborator: Christo — Cubase → FL Studio → Studio One (S-57); "trial-and-error mindset"; modern drums over soul samples (S-40, S-58). Album credits across the era include DJ Scheme, Nuri, Nice Rec, Hollywood JB, Kenny Beats, Mac Miller (post-production/arrangement), Monte Booker, Kaytranada, BADBADNOTGOOD, Thundercat, Pluss, Boi-1da, Cubeatz, Jay Versace, Lex Luger, Childish Major (S-48, S-52, S-53, S-59, S-60).
- ChaseTheMoney (DiCaprio-era hit): FL Studio ~10 years, "browses for a random sound and keeps it if he likes it", many BPMs/drum patterns, "dark and radical" St. Louis production, Three 6 Mafia influence (S-45, S-46, S-55, S-56).
- Techniques (general knowledge, `[infer]`): chopping 60s–70s soul/gospel on an FL Studio slicer, pitching samples down 2–5 semitones and low-passing them, layering a cracking acoustic snare over a trap clap, hi-hat velocity randomization + triplet rolls, 808 through soft-clip/saturation, arrangement built from 2–3 unrelated loops stitched with risers.

## LANE 4 — phonk-experimental (original / SoundCloud phonk, NOT drift phonk)

### (a) Sonic signature

**Tempo — the two counts.**
- Memphis/classic phonk is counted at **130–150 BPM** (several guides, S-64, S-65, S-70) with the backbeat in half-time, so it *feels* like **65–75 BPM**; one guide places the swung-hip-hop feel at 80–100 (S-65). The chopped-and-screwed source material was slowed to "60 to 70 quarter-note beats per minute" (S-66).
- Drift phonk runs 140–170 BPM (S-64, S-67, S-70) and is counted straight with a driving cowbell lead.
- Working range for our lane: **state "70 BPM half-time feel" or "140 BPM, half-time, slow swung groove"**; for screwed variants "65 BPM, slowed". A Suno community prompt for the exact aesthetic used "Memphis underground rap, chopped and screwed, phonk … 72 BPM, tape-worn drum loops, sparse intro" (P-18).

**Drums & groove.** Drum-machine drums from the Boss DR-660 / Roland TR-808 family (S-69, S-71): boomy, long-decay 808 kick, dry snappy 808 snare or clap, closed hats in straight 8ths or shuffled 16ths, open hat on the off-beat, and the **808 cowbell as a rhythmic accent, not a melody** — in the original Memphis style the cowbell plays syncopated 16th patterns and "pitched 808 cowbells … mapped at various pitches to create melodies" only in sparse, two- or three-note call-and-response figures (S-71), not the continuous saw-like lead of drift phonk. Off-grid swing from looping chopped loops with imperfect cut points; drums frequently come from a sampled, pitched-down Memphis tape so they inherit wobble and hiss. Programming is sparse; fills are hat rolls and cowbell flourishes rather than snare fills.

**Bass.** Distorted / saturated 808 with long decay; Memphis producers "pitched multiple 808 kicks with long decay times to create basslines" (S-71); heavy low-pass warmth, bit-crushing, tape saturation. Not the clipped, sidechained drift-phonk 808. Occasional sampled upright/electric bass from the jazz or soul loop.

**Harmony / keys / modes.** Dark natural minor and Phrygian; two-chord or single-riff loops; eerie minor-key keys/strings from slowed horror-soundtrack or 70s soul records; slowed jazz piano/Rhodes and funk/soul samples ("slowed-down funk and soul samples are, of course, magical", S-72; "jazzy, calmer samples" and "lo-fi hip hop and jazzy riffs" cited for the pioneers, S-73, S-74). Low registers dominate because everything is pitched down.

**Lead instruments / textures.** Chopped-and-screwed vocal textures used as instruments: pitched-down (-3 to -7 semitones), slowed, chopped, repeated phrases turned into rhythmic stabs; lyrics "often unrecognizable" (S-75) — in our instrumental lane describe these as *pitched-down vocal-sample texture, chopped, no intelligible words*, or replace with "pitched-down sample stabs"; eerie 80s/90s synth pads, horror-movie keys, sampled jazz piano and Rhodes, funk guitar snippets, SP-1200-style 12-bit sample grit (S-71), sparse bell/clave tones made from an 808 clave with maxed decay (S-71), air-raid/siren one-shots, record scratches.

**Textures & FX.** Tape hiss, vinyl crackle, cassette wow and flutter, bitcrush, low-pass filtered everything, heavy reverb/tape delay on sample stabs ("drench them in reverb and tape delay", S-65), VHS/“VCR in a trash compactor” low end (S-76), muddy intentional lo-fi mastering; crowd/static noise beds; DJ Screw-style record slowdowns and double-copy "chop" repeats (S-66, S-77).

**Arrangement habits.** Loop-based, static: 8-bar intro of the sample alone (often with tape start-up), drums in, cowbell/hat variations every 8 bars, a breakdown to the bare sample, drums back; few true drops; tracks fade or tape-stop. "Sparse intro" is community-reported phrasing (P-18).

**Mix & master.** Deliberately lo-fi: low-passed top, crushed dynamics from tape/bit reduction, wide hissy noise floor, 808 dominating the low end, mono-ish center with hazy wide reverb tails; loudness moderate — intentionally murky rather than radio-bright. "Low bass, hard drums, eerie keys, and a lo-fi sense of space" is how Suno's own genre page frames Memphis phonk (P-20). For our "radio-ready" goal, keep the lo-fi *texture* but ask for a clean, controlled sub and a modern master (see cross-lane section) — "polished grit".

**Era / genre influences cited.** 1990s Memphis rap tapes (Three 6 Mafia circle, Tommy Wright III, DJ Spanish Fly, DJ Squeeky, DJ Zirk — S-69, S-79, S-80); Houston chopped-and-screwed (S-66, S-77); the 2010–2012 lo-fi Memphis-revival scene that coined the term (Raider Klan era; "dark, lo-fi production style", S-76, S-81); mid-2010s SoundCloud phonk (DJ Smokey, Soudiere, DJ Yung Vamp, Mythic — "Memphis roots … maintained by [the first two]", "simple … fewer layers and more readily-available sounds" for the third, S-82, S-83); jazz and funk samples (S-75); the term "phonk" as slang for Memphis rap / funk (S-75).

**How it differs from drift phonk (what to exclude).** Drift phonk (late-2010s Russia/TikTok) is defined by "the use of cowbells as a melodic element and loud, persistent electronic bass" (S-75), "stripped away vocal samples, cranked the BPM (140–160), and went all-in on aggressive cowbell patterns and distorted 808s" (S-67); it is counted straight, uses sidechained EDM-style pumping, big risers/drops, and increasingly Brazilian-funk (baile) rhythms and gym/edit energy (S-84). "Some phonk purists don't consider drift phonk 'real' phonk, since it shares little of the original Southern hip-hop DNA" (S-67). Our lane keeps: slowed half-time feel, sampled jazz/soul/Memphis material, tape texture, cowbell as accent. Our lane excludes: cowbell melody lead, straight 150-BPM drive, sidechain pump, EDM builds/drops, Brazilian funk percussion, "gym/drift/car edit" energy.

### (b) Producers / gear / techniques cited (understanding only)
- Memphis 90s gear: Boss DR-660 (core drum machine, all sounds pitch-mappable), Boss DR-5 (1993+, more melodic), E-mu SP-1200 (12-bit, dirtier when pitched), Gemini DS-1224 lo-fi sampler, Roland TR-808, turntables; cassette 4-track distribution (S-69, S-71, S-79). Techniques: pitched 808 cowbell/clave melodies, stacked long-decay 808 kicks as basslines, clave with max decay as a bell (S-71).
- Houston chopped-and-screwed: two copies of a record, one slowed (screwed) and one beat-juggled (chopped), 60–70 BPM (S-66, S-77).
- 2010s SoundCloud phonk: FL Studio ("FL Studio" was the one-word answer from the genre's most-cited producer, S-72); sample-first workflow — "spends whole days cutting samples … to build up sound banks" (S-72); slowed funk/soul samples (S-73); project files passed back and forth between collaborators (S-72).
- Techniques (general knowledge, `[infer]`): pitch samples down 3–7 semitones with tape-style time/pitch linkage, low-pass at 8–12 kHz, add vinyl/cassette noise beds, bitcrush to 12-bit, long-decay 808 with soft saturation, cowbell as 16th-note accent at low velocity, reverb/tape-delay throws on sample stabs.
