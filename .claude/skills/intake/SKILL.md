# Intake

The conversation that turns an empty Hearth into a useful one.

## The rule that makes this work

**Keep it under five minutes.** A forty-question onboarding wizard is why people
abandon this category of product. Gather only what is needed to be useful
*tomorrow morning*, then learn everything else by watching.

Every question you ask is a question you are admitting you could not figure out
yourself. Before asking anything, check whether the calendar, the inbox, or
`memory/` already answers it. Ask about what recurs — the repeating skeleton of
the week and the year — because that is what cannot be inferred from a single
day's data and what pays off most.

## Order

Ask in this order and stop when you have enough to run a morning brief. If the
person's attention is going, stop early and continue another day; a half-filled
memory that gets used beats a complete one that annoyed someone into quitting.

1. **The week's skeleton.** What happens every week and on which day — classes,
   practices, standing work commitments, the recurring workout. Who each one
   belongs to, and who does the driving. This alone makes the morning brief real.

2. **The clock.** City and timezone, when the morning brief should land, and what
   time the household actually leaves on a school morning. Ask for the real
   number, not the intended one.

3. **The money calendar.** Only the timing, never the amounts unless offered:
   which days people get paid, what comes out automatically and when. Then say
   back the one thing they came for — where a debit lands before a paycheck.

4. **The seasonal stuff.** Signups, registrations, renewals, memberships that come
   round once or twice a year and get missed. Ask what they nearly missed last
   year; that question surfaces more than a list of categories does.

5. **Food.** How many nights actually get cooked, which nights are too rushed,
   what nobody will eat, and the handful of meals that always work.

6. **The recurring failure.** "What goes wrong most weeks?" Ask this last, when
   they are warmed up. It is the single most valuable answer in the interview,
   and the first working version should be aimed straight at it.

## Writing it down

As answers arrive, write them to the right layer — this is the whole point of the
interview, and getting the layer wrong is how memory rots later:

- Static truths → `memory/facts.md` (marked `told`, not `observed`)
- Anything with a clock → `memory/rhythms.md` with cadence, lead time and action
- Anything promised during the interview → `memory/open-loops.md`
- Roster, timezone, city, brief time → `CLAUDE.md`

Mark everything from intake as **told**, not observed. Told facts are a starting
hypothesis; the first few weeks of watching are what confirm or correct them. When
observation contradicts something you were told, that is a correction — record it,
do not silently overwrite.

## Adding a person later

Do not re-run the whole interview. Ask only what is theirs: their week, their
recurring commitments, how they want to be reached. Everything about the household
is already known.

## What not to do

- Do not ask for anything you can read from a connected calendar or inbox.
- Do not ask about health, money beyond timing, or tensions between people.
- Do not present this as a form. It is a conversation; follow what they bring up
  and come back to your order afterwards.
- Do not finish by promising everything at once. Say the one or two things Hearth
  will do tomorrow morning, and let it earn the rest.
